Know the threats

Phishing

The most common trick online, and one of the easiest to learn to spot.

What is it?

What is phishing?

Phishing is when someone pretends to be a person or company you trust (a bank, a school, a delivery service, even a friend) to trick you into giving away passwords, codes or personal details, or into clicking something harmful.

The name comes from "fishing": attackers throw out bait, a message, and wait to see who bites.

Why it matters

One click can be enough

A single successful phishing message can give someone access to your accounts, your money or your identity. It works because it targets people, not technology, so knowing the warning signs is your best defence.

How does it happen?

Where phishing shows up

✉️

Email

Fake alerts, invoices or "password expired" notices that copy real logos.

💬

Text and chat

Short messages with a link, often about parcels, prizes or account problems.

👥

Social media

Hacked or fake accounts that send a shocking link or an offer that is too good to be true.

🌐

Fake websites

Pages that look like a real login screen but send what you type to the attacker.

How do I recognize it?

Spot the red flags

Here is a made-up phishing message with the warning signs marked.

📧

Account Support <help@accounts-secure.example>1

Action required

Dear user, your account will be locked in 2 hours2. Confirm your password3 here:

secure-login-check.example/verify4
  1. A strange sender address. It doesn't match the real company's website.
  2. Pressure and urgency. A deadline is meant to stop you thinking.
  3. A request for your password. Real services won't ask for it by message.
  4. A look-alike link. It leads to a fake page, not the real site.

How do I protect myself?

Before you click, and after

Before you click

  • Check the sender's address, not just the display name
  • Slow down: urgency is a tactic
  • Open the official app, or type the website address yourself
  • Preview links first (hover on a computer, press and hold on a phone)
  • Never share passwords or one-time codes
  • Use two-factor authentication as a safety net

If you already clicked or typed something

  1. Stop. Don't enter anything else or open attachments.
  2. Change your password from the official app or site, not the link, and anywhere you reused it.
  3. Turn on two-factor authentication and sign out of other devices.
  4. Check for activity you don't recognize.
  5. Report the message to the platform, your school or your IT team.

When in doubt, don't use the link. Open the official app or type the website address yourself, then check whether the message is real.
